Setting: Late afternoon at Hafer Park. The heat sits heavy over the walking path.

Marisa: (fanning herself with her hand) Marisol, I can feel this heat in my chest already. Should we really keep going?
Marisol: We can turn back. The girls can play near the shade instead — that's enough exercise for one afternoon.
Marisa: (checking her phone) My watch just buzzed. Air quality alert — ozone is high again today, same as last week.
Marisol: That explains it. This kind of heat can sneak up on you fast, especially with bad air on top of it.
Marisa: Camy! Donna! Come here a second, mijas.

Camy jogs over, a little out of breath, rubbing her throat.

Camy: Mami, my throat feels scratchy. Can we get some water?
Marisol: (touching Camy's forehead, then glancing at Marisa) Of course, mi amor. We can cut the walk short today — better safe than sorry.
Marisa: Let's head home. We should stay indoors for the rest of the evening, close the windows, and let this air quality alert pass.
Marisol: Good idea. Tomorrow, if that throat is still bothering her, I can bring her by the clinic before my shift.

# === END VOCAB TUESDAY ===

Tuesday morning, the waiting room fills faster than usual. Marisol brings Camy in before her own shift starts; Marisa arrives ten minutes later with Donna, both girls rubbing their throats and coughing a raspy cough that wasn't there yesterday.

Dra. Pérez calls them back together.

Dra. Pérez: Two girls, same symptoms, same afternoon at the park. That is not a coincidence — that is ozone.
Marisa: My mother always said fresh outdoor air is good for the lungs, no matter what. Is that just an old wives' tale now?
Dra. Pérez: (smiling gently) It used to be true more often than it isn't now. Fresh air is good — but on a day with an air quality alert, the "fresh" air outside actually has more ozone in it than the air in your living room. It's not about the outdoors being bad. It's about knowing which days to skip it.
Marisol: So this dry throat and cough — that's the ozone, not a cold?
Dra. Pérez: Most likely, yes. Ozone irritates the airways the same way smoke does. It should clear up on its own with rest, fluids, and indoor air over the next day or two. If she starts wheezing, breathes fast, or seems unusually tired, that's when you should bring her straight back — those are different from a simple irritation.
Marisa: Should we keep them home from school tomorrow?
Dra. Pérez: You can send them if they're comfortable and breathing normally. Just watch how they do outside at recess — if the school has an air quality alert posted, they should stay in for that hour, same as we will.
Marisol: (relieved) A rule of thumb I can actually remember. Gracias, doctora.

# === END VOCAB THURSDAY ===

Coffee is still hot in the breakroom when Dra. Pérez gathers Amanda, Rosie, Letia, and Marisol around the table.

Dra. Pérez: The health department sent an alert. Measles cases are up in the region, earlier than usual this year. We must be ready.
Amanda: Doctora, I already had two calls this week from parents asking if the vaccine is even safe. One said her cousin told her it causes autism.
Dra. Pérez: That myth has been studied for over twenty years now, in many different countries, with millions of children. It doesn't hold up — the fact is the vaccine prevents a disease that can be very dangerous, especially for babies.
Rosie: So what do we tell parents who are scared?
Dra. Pérez: We tell them the truth calmly, without judging them for asking. Fear spreads faster than facts sometimes — our job is to slow that down. If a patient calls with a fever and a rash, they must not sit in the waiting room. Amanda, you must screen them by phone first.
Letia: And for families who are behind on the MMR vaccine?
Dra. Pérez: We should check every record carefully, not assume the worst. If a child needs a dose, we schedule it — no lecture, no shame. That's how herd immunity works: enough of us protected, so the disease can't find a foothold.
Marisol: (thinking of Camy) I should double check her vaccine card tonight, just to be sure.
Dra. Pérez: That's exactly the right instinct.

It was worth it, Marisol thinks, to ask questions instead of guessing.
